1A place to begin

Welcome! My name is Edgardo Civallero. I write from the mist — from the cloud forest and páramo of Cundinamarca, Colombia. I'm a librarian, archivist, musician, writer, and visual artist — and work as a semantic consultant, researcher, editor, designer, speaker, teacher, naturalist, and memory weaver.

This site brings together my work across librarianship, archives, sound, visual arts, biology / ecology, and the stewardship of knowledge and memory — fields where information braids with silence, research touches rot, and musical instruments (and sometimes the puppets from my workshop) argue with metadata.

Use the sidebar to navigate by theme. Browse, read, wander. And if any thread hums back to you — write to me. May your path be a good one.

3Chronicles of Things Made

A log of recent writings, sounds, and gestures

Published July 12, 2026

Over the past two weeks (June 29 to July 12), four new texts moved across marginalized voices, Pacific navigation, forest regeneration, and frost in the high Andes. Their terrains differ, but each examines a system whose survival depends on relations that institutions often overlook: the authority to interpret, the practices that make an object legible, the renewal of knowledge across change, and the margins that allow a fragile structure to endure sudden pressure.

The sequence begins with Listening to the Margins, an essay on community-centered librarianship and the institutional organization of inaudibility. Marginalized communities do not lack voices; they are made difficult to hear through categories, procedures, formats, and structures of authority that determine whose speech becomes intelligible or consequential. The text distinguishes listening from symbolic inclusion and asks what happens when communities are recognized not merely as sources of testimony, but as producers of interpretation capable of changing collections, description, access, evaluation, and governance.

The Sea That Had to Be Remembered turns to Marshallese stick charts and a documentary tradition formed around waves, islands, memory, and trained bodily perception. These charts were teaching devices rather than self-contained maps consulted during a voyage. They prepared navigators to recognize relations that had to be memorized and later encountered through the movement of the canoe and the sea. Their survival in museums reveals the limits of preserving an object after the practices, authorities, routes, and interpretive relations that once made it operational have been disrupted.

The question of continuity shifts again in The Forest Does Not Store Itself. A forest persists through succession, decomposition, inheritance, and replacement, not by retaining each of its components unchanged. The chronicle uses this regenerative logic to examine libraries, archives, and museums whose collections may remain physically secure while the descriptive, custodial, technical, and ethical capacities around them weaken. Preservation therefore requires succession, recoverable decision-making, layered records, and the renewal of the relations through which stored materials remain intelligible and accountable.

Finally, From Quisquiza | Frost returns to the high-Andean forest, where a few hours of cold can collapse seedlings, crack roof tiles, and reveal differences in exposure that remained invisible under favorable conditions. Frost becomes a way of examining institutions that appear stable while operating close to their limits. A small disruption may receive the blame for failure, although the threshold was built gradually through exhaustion, deferred maintenance, concentrated knowledge, and the disappearance of alternatives. What looked redundant before the cold arrived may prove to have been the condition of survival.

Across these four texts, continuity emerges as a demanding practice rather than a passive condition. Listening requires the redistribution of interpretive authority. A chart remains meaningful through teaching, memory, movement, and use. A collection continues through regenerated capacities rather than storage alone. A system survives pressure because someone preserved a margin: another route, another record, another person who knows.

The posts trace knowledge through the relations that keep it alive. Voices must be allowed to reorganize the institutions that hear them. Documents must remain connected to the practices that make them legible. Preservation must prepare for succession and transformation. Resilience must include space for what cannot be reduced to immediate efficiency. What endures does so through authority, practice, renewal, and room to breathe.


4Coming Soon, If the Fog Allows

Upcoming writings, events, and works-in-progress

Published April 21, 2026

Over the coming months, new threads will begin to surface here — if conditions allow.

One of them is the relaunch of Wayrachaki Editora, an independent imprint devoted to open-access digital books written from the margins: manuals, chronicles, and editorial texts on libraries in resistance, insurgent orality, fragile archives, and knowledge practices shaped by conflict, scarcity, and territory. The imprint resumes its work with the same nomadic spirit that first gave it form, publishing texts that would never survive academic vetting and were never meant to.

Another thread takes shape through Tela de Araña, a set of consulting and advisory services focused on semantic design, metadata systems, community archives, and knowledge infrastructures where conventional models fail. This work unfolds slowly and situationally, through accompaniment rather than solutions, and will be documented here as it happens.

Alongside these developments, the chronicles, notes, and essays will continue. Writing will keep moving between forests and libraries, between theory and ground, following questions as they arise rather than as they are planned. Nothing here is scheduled in advance. Some things will arrive quietly. Others may never appear.

Fog, after all, has its own timing.


5Collected Texts, Sounds & Traces

Archive of published works and recorded materials

Published April 21, 2026

Over time, I have been reorgaizing my earlier publications into a structured archive. Texts once dispersed across journals, conferences, and digital platforms now inhabit defined sections that reflect the terrains from which they emerged. The archive currently includes Libraries from the South, Libraries in the Margins, Public Libraries, Librarianship and Research, and Digital Divide, alongside curated selections from Blog Bibliotecario, Bitácora de un bibliotecario, and the Princh Library Blog.

These sections are not a museum of completed work. They remain active spaces: updated, expanded, and occasionally recontextualized as questions evolve and threads reconnect. Together, they trace the long arc of a professional trajectory concerned with decolonial critique, marginal territories, institutional responsibility, technological inequality, documentary practice, and the politics of memory.

What appears in the present writing grows from those earlier layers. The archive is not a backdrop. It is the foundation.
