Community-Centered Librarianship With a Decolonial Turn (10)

Libraries as Tools for Radical Education

Turning Information into Collective Inquiry and Action

 

This post is part of a series that reclaims community-centered librarianship from its institutional distortions, returning it to its roots in struggle, mutual aid, and collective survival. It treats libraries not as neutral services but as contested infrastructures shaped by power, resistance, and memory, and explores librarianship as solidarity work grounded in real communities, real conflicts, and the ongoing tension between institutional control and collective autonomy. A Workshop Is Not Yet a Pedagogy

Imagine that several residents arrive at a library carrying copies of the same eviction notice. The library can direct them to housing law, help them search court records, invite a legal adviser, or prepare a guide explaining the relevant procedures. Each response may be useful. Yet all of them can leave the underlying educational relation unchanged: the library possesses or obtains knowledge, specialists explain it, and residents receive it.

A different process begins when the notice becomes an object of collective investigation. The group reads its language closely. Participants compare dates, clauses, signatures, and inconsistencies. They identify who issued it, which laws it invokes, what it leaves unexplained, and whether similar notices have appeared elsewhere. Personal experience, legal sources, property records, local reporting, and previous cases are brought into relation. Questions that no one can answer immediately become part of the work rather than evidence that the session has failed.

The educational character of the encounter depends on what happens to authority inside it. A lecture on revolutionary literature can reproduce obedience. A session on an apparently ordinary administrative document can develop collective analytical power. The relation created among people, evidence, and interpretation determines its radical force.

The decisive change occurs when people cease to be the audience for information and become investigators of a situation they need to understand.

 

The Problem Comes Before the Lesson

Paulo Freire described conventional education through the image of banking: knowledge is deposited in learners who are expected to receive, retain, and reproduce it. His alternative, problem-posing education, begins with a reality that learners already inhabit but have not yet been able to interpret fully.

For libraries, this changes how educational work is designed. A ready-made class with predetermined conclusions gives way to an unresolved situation: perhaps a rent increase, unpaid wages, the closure of a health service, or a contaminated stream. The immediate problem supplies the first questions, while library resources help the group enlarge and sharpen them.

A useful session might place a municipal ordinance beside a map, a newspaper report, a budget table, a photograph, and testimony from people directly affected. Participants can trace where each claim originated, compare official explanations with observable conditions, and identify gaps that require further research. The librarian contributes skills in searching, verification, source comparison, document interpretation, and information organization. Those skills support the inquiry without predetermining its outcome.

Dialogue here is disciplined work. Participants still need to distinguish an allegation from documented evidence and a plausible inference from an established fact. Technical language may require explanation. Conflicting claims need examination. Rigor remains essential, and its production becomes a shared and visible process.

The lesson therefore grows from the problem. Its direction remains open long enough for new evidence, disagreement, and experience to change what the group thinks it knows.

 

Study Must Return to the Situation

Freire's idea of praxis joins reflection to action. In library education, that connection prevents critical inquiry from ending as an interesting conversation.

The residents studying an eviction notice may decide to create a comparison sheet, reconstruct a timeline, prepare questions for a housing office, locate other affected tenants, or bring their findings to a lawyer. A group investigating water contamination may design a sampling log, compare official monitoring sites with community observations, or assemble the evidence required to demand an independent test. The educational process has produced something that can be used.

Action gives the group new material to examine. An agency may answer evasively, a legal argument may prove weaker than expected, or a map may reveal an unnoticed pattern. A proposed tactic may also expose some participants to greater risk than others. The group returns to the library, studies what happened, and revises its analysis.

This rhythm requires continuity. A single event can introduce a subject, but it rarely allows participants to investigate, act, assess the consequences, and begin again. Libraries can support the process by keeping source trails, reserving space for subsequent meetings, maintaining working copies of documents, and ensuring that the methods used in one inquiry remain available for the next.

A strong process reduces dependence on the librarian or invited expert. People leave better able to locate evidence, test an explanation, recognize uncertainty, and organize another investigation themselves.

 

Political Education Without Political Tutelage

Frantz Fanon treated political education as essential to decolonization, while warning against leaders who confuse education with speeches, mobilization, or the management of an obedient public. That warning applies directly to radical library programs.

A library can adopt the language of resistance and still reproduce tutelage. Staff may select the correct books, invite speakers who deliver the correct analysis, and guide participants toward conclusions already decided. The community is no longer treated as politically passive, yet it remains an audience for somebody else's line.

Political education demands more difficult work. Participants need access to the decisions, interests, institutions, and material forces shaping a conflict. They need to understand how a policy was produced, who can alter it, which alliances sustain it, where pressure can be applied, and what each possible response may cost. They also need room to dispute strategy without being accused of insufficient commitment.

This is why the language of "arming communities with knowledge" must be handled carefully. The metaphor imagines an enlightened library handing over a weapon. In practice, a document acquires political force when people can interpret it, connect it to their circumstances, challenge the uses made of it, and decide what to do with what they have learned.

Fanon also brings material conditions into view. A study process scheduled during working hours or conducted in an inaccessible language distributes participation unequally. Transport, food, disability, childcare, and personal safety belong to pedagogical planning because they shape who can sustain the work.

 

Engaged Pedagogy Changes the Facilitator

In Teaching to Transgress, bell hooks develops an engaged pedagogy in which learning involves the whole person and changes teachers as well as students. The risk, openness, and intellectual movement expected from participants must also reach the facilitator rather than stopping at the boundary of professional detachment.

For librarians, engaged presence can coexist with professional boundaries and expertise. Staff must be prepared to explain why they selected particular materials, admit when they do not know something, recognize when a participant's experience unsettles their interpretation, and revise the session accordingly. Their authority becomes visible and answerable rather than disappearing behind the program.

hooks also refuses the idea that serious political education must be joyless. Anger, grief, humor, pleasure, fatigue, curiosity, and fear all affect how people learn. A group examining violence or dispossession cannot be treated as a collection of disembodied readers. At the same time, no one should have to disclose painful experience in order to earn a place in the discussion.

An engaged library session can offer different ways of entering the work. Some participants may annotate a text or draw a map; others may work through a practical demonstration, a paired analysis, or collective debate. These options make thought possible across different ways of speaking, concentrating, remembering, and responding without manufacturing emotional intensity.

The facilitator shares responsibility for the intellectual and human conditions of the encounter. That responsibility includes accepting that the learning process may alter the person who convened it.

 

The Lesson Can Remain Unsettled

Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui grounds decolonization in decolonizing practices. Her sociology of the image also challenges the assumption that critical thought lives primarily in written argument. Photographs and drawings, along with gestures, clothing, streets, and domestic arrangements, can disclose colonial relations that official language conceals.

A library inquiry shaped by that provocation brings images into the analysis from the beginning. Participants might examine how a redevelopment plan depicts an "empty" neighborhood, compare promotional renderings with photographs of existing life, or trace what has been removed from a map. The visual record becomes a field of argument.

Rivera Cusicanqui's concept of ch'ixi, developed from an Aymara intellectual and historical ground, also resists the demand that contradiction must end in a harmonious synthesis. Its value here is precise: a library group can learn together while several irreducible accounts remain in tension.

Official water data may conflict with residents' sensory observations and fishers' seasonal calendars. Preserving those differences avoids choosing one source as reality and reducing the rest to culture or perception. Placed beside one another, they may reveal where institutions measure, where residents experience, and where each account remains incomplete.

Turning ch'ixi into portable library jargon would strip it from the world in which Rivera Cusicanqui developed it. Her work can instead interrupt a familiar pedagogical compulsion: the desire to resolve difference, summarize conflict, and finish every session with a coherent position. Some learning consists in making a contradiction more exact and carrying it forward without pretending it has disappeared.

 

The Curriculum Hidden in Ordinary Library Work

Ordinary encounters provide much of the raw material for a radical curriculum. Its form depends on how the library organizes them.

At the reference desk, a person can receive an answer and leave, or can be shown how the answer was assembled: which terms opened the search, why one source was trusted over another, where uncertainty remains, and how the trail can be followed independently. The transaction becomes a small apprenticeship in inquiry.

A reading group can move beyond discussion of a book's themes by placing the text beside the documents of the world it describes. A novel about migration might be read with a border regulation, a transport map, a family photograph, employment statistics, and a migrant association's newsletter. Literature then helps participants perceive what administrative sources cannot express, while those sources expose the structures surrounding individual experience.

Digital literacy can examine more than how to operate a device or platform. Participants can investigate what the platform collects, how an interface directs behavior, which results a search engine ranks, what an automated system claims to infer, and how an account can be protected. Technical competence grows together with an understanding of the environment in which the technology operates.

Even collection displays can be organized as invitations to inquiry. Instead of assembling books that announce a settled theme, a display can place conflicting accounts together, identify the questions separating them, and offer a path into further research. The shelf becomes the beginning of a problem rather than its official explanation.

These practices require preparation, judgment, and follow-through. Their radical quality lies in the intellectual relations they create: people gain access to the means by which claims are examined and knowledge is made usable.

 

What Learning Makes Possible

Libraries bring unusual resources to radical education. A question can remain open across time and connect a conversation to documents, technologies, spaces, and people with different forms of expertise. The trail of inquiry can survive long enough for participants to return after action has produced new evidence. That continuity makes methods repeatable.

None of this guarantees liberation. Educational work takes place inside conflicts it cannot resolve on its own. A community may understand a system clearly and still lack the power to change it. Participants may disagree about what action is desirable. Institutions may ignore evidence, defend their interests, or retaliate.

The value of the process lies in the capacities it builds under those conditions. People learn to formulate sharper questions, compare sources, expose a misleading explanation, and reconsider a course of action in light of its consequences. Knowledge becomes a collective practice rather than a finished object transferred from one person to another.

A shelf of radical books proves very little. The work begins when a community can turn a problem into a question, a question into an inquiry, and an inquiry into something it can do together.
