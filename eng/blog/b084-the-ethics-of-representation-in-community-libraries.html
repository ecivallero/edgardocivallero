Community-Centered Librarianship With a Decolonial Turn (09)

The Ethics of Representation in Community Libraries

Who Speaks for a Community, and Who Controls What Is Said?

 

This post is part of a series that reclaims community-centered librarianship from its institutional distortions, returning it to its roots in struggle, mutual aid, and collective survival. It treats libraries not as neutral services but as contested infrastructures shaped by power, resistance, and memory, and explores librarianship as solidarity work grounded in real communities, real conflicts, and the ongoing tension between institutional control and collective autonomy. Representation as an Exercise of Authority

Community libraries are often expected to represent the people around them. Their collections should reflect local lives, their programs should make marginalized histories visible, and their public language should recognize identities neglected by larger institutions. Representation, under this view, appears to correct an absence.

Yet making a community visible involves more than placing its materials on a shelf or including its members in an exhibition. Someone determines which people, experiences, objects, and events will stand for the community. Someone chooses the words used to describe them. Someone establishes the context in which they will be encountered and decides what will remain outside the frame.

Those decisions produce a public version of the community. Once incorporated into a catalog, website, exhibition, publication, oral history collection, or institutional report, that version can circulate far beyond the relationships in which it was created. It may influence how journalists, researchers, public agencies, educators, funders, and future residents understand the people represented there.

The ethical problem therefore begins with authority. Who initiated the representation? Who selected its contents? Who interpreted them? Who approved the result? Who benefits from its circulation, and who must live with its consequences?

Good intentions cannot answer these questions. A sympathetic portrayal can still misidentify people, expose protected knowledge, flatten disagreement, or turn a political struggle into a reassuring story of cultural diversity. Representation acquires ethical legitimacy through the conditions under which it is produced and controlled.

 

The Intermediary's Power

Librarians frequently occupy an intermediary position. They arrange interviews, select photographs, write captions, prepare exhibitions, edit community publications, create catalog records, and translate local descriptions into professional metadata. Their work connects community knowledge with systems capable of preserving and circulating it.

That position carries interpretive power.

An oral history may last two hours, while the exhibition uses three sentences. A person may describe an experience through local terms that have no direct equivalent in the catalog, yet the record requires a subject heading. A neighborhood conflict may involve decades of disagreement, while the public program needs a concise introduction. Even careful professional work compresses, orders, and frames.

The danger appears when mediation is presented as transparent. Institutional language can make the librarian's intervention disappear, leaving the resulting description to appear natural or collectively authorized. A text written by staff becomes "the community's story." A selection made for an exhibition becomes "community memory." Several interviews are synthesized into a single narrative whose editorial construction remains invisible.

Librarians cannot avoid interpretation by claiming neutrality. They can, however, expose their own role in the process. Editorial decisions can be documented. Contributors can review how their words are quoted and contextualized. Uncertainty can remain visible rather than being resolved for narrative convenience. Catalog records can distinguish community-provided descriptions from terminology supplied by the library.

Professional mediation becomes more accountable when its interventions can be identified, questioned, and revised. Without that accountability, the intermediary may gradually become the authorized interpreter of experiences that do not belong to them.

 

Who Can Speak for a Community?

The phrase "community voice" often conceals disagreement. Communities contain different generations, political positions, religious affiliations, migration histories, family networks, economic interests, and degrees of local authority. Their members may disagree sharply about the past, about who belongs, or about what should be made public.

Institutional projects nevertheless tend to seek recognizable representatives. Librarians may approach elected leaders, respected elders, association presidents, cultural organizers, teachers, activists, or people already accustomed to working with institutions. These individuals may possess genuine authority, but their visibility does not grant them the ability to speak for everyone.

Representation becomes especially difficult when local authority is unequal. A neighborhood leader may exclude recent arrivals. A cultural organization may promote one version of collective identity while dismissing internal minorities. Elders may hold knowledge that younger members contest. Men may speak publicly about histories in which women carried much of the work. Those able to attend meetings may have more influence than people constrained by employment, disability, caregiving, language, fear, or insecure legal status.

Adding more speakers does not automatically resolve the problem. Knowledge may carry different forms of authorization. A person can describe their own experience without being entitled to disclose another person's history. An individual may possess knowledge whose circulation is governed collectively. Public recognition within the community may differ from the authority to discuss a specific subject.

A serious process must establish what each contributor is authorized to represent and whose interests may be affected. It should also preserve disagreement when no shared account exists. Producing a coherent institutional narrative by erasing conflict gives the library clarity at the cost of the community's complexity.

 

Extraction Through Participation

Community representation often depends on contributions: interviews, family photographs, personal papers, translations, testimonies, historical explanations, volunteer labor, and introductions to other participants. These contributions can create valuable collections. They can also support an extractive relationship.

The imbalance is easy to miss because the project may be collaborative and warmly received. Residents contribute time and knowledge; the library acquires documentary content, professional recognition, material for grant reports, and a stronger public profile. Contributors may receive acknowledgment, an invitation to the opening event, or digital copies of their materials. Control over preservation, description, access, and later reuse usually remains with the institution.

Consent does not eliminate this imbalance when people have been given only a general account of the project. Agreeing to an interview is different from agreeing to unrestricted online publication. Allowing a photograph to appear in a local exhibition does not necessarily authorize its use in publicity, educational packages, commercial publications, automated datasets, or unrelated future projects. A broad release form can transfer extensive rights without creating shared understanding.

Before materials are collected, contributors need clear information about custody, ownership, access, reproduction, attribution, editing, and foreseeable reuse. They should know whether files will be placed online, whether users can download them, whether the institution intends to license them, and whether restrictions can be changed later. Where a project relies on extensive community expertise, compensation and control deserve the same attention as acknowledgment.

The distribution of benefits also belongs to the ethical assessment. A project may preserve important testimony while leaving the contributors unable to access the resulting collection because of technical barriers, institutional location, or language. Community members may provide knowledge that later becomes available mainly to researchers. An institution can celebrate participation while accumulating resources that the contributors cannot govern.

Extraction occurs when relationships are converted into institutional assets without a corresponding transfer of authority, capacity, or lasting benefit.

 

When Living Communities Become Heritage

Representation can turn living people into objects of cultural display. Community exhibitions often favor photographs, celebrations, crafts, food, music, oral traditions, and stories of migration or hardship. These materials can carry deep meaning. Their arrangement can also create a community that appears culturally distinctive, historically complete, and politically harmless.

Musealization begins when the institution treats a living social world as a finished cultural object. Practices are detached from current conflicts and presented as survivals from the past. Poverty becomes resilience. Displacement becomes migration heritage. Labor struggle becomes local history. Cultural difference becomes an attractive identity that visitors can appreciate without confronting the conditions under which that difference has been marginalized.

The resulting portrayal may be affectionate and still produce harm. Communities are expected to appear traditional enough to be recognizable, cohesive enough to be representable, and resilient enough to be celebrated. Internal change can then look like cultural loss, while anger and political disagreement appear to disturb the exhibition's intended message.

Community libraries should resist the pressure to create definitive portraits. Collections and exhibitions can identify their scope, acknowledge what they cannot represent, and show how their selections were made. Historical materials need connections to present conditions where those connections remain active. Contemporary community members should retain the ability to revise inherited descriptions and challenge portrayals created in their name.

Living communities exceed every record made about them. Ethical representation preserves that excess instead of presenting the collection as a complete account.

 

Description and Circulation

The representational process continues after materials have been collected. Titles, captions, subject terms, summaries, image crops, keywords, dates, and arrangement determine how records will be found and understood. A harmful description can persist even when the item itself has been preserved carefully.

Institutional vocabularies may reproduce names imposed by governments, researchers, police, welfare agencies, missionaries, landowners, or the press. Historical documents may contain racist, sexist, colonial, or stigmatizing language. Replacing every offensive term can obscure evidence of how the record was created, while retaining it without explanation can reproduce the injury and make the imposed terminology the principal point of access.

There is no universal formula for resolving that tension. Description can preserve the original title as historical evidence while adding contextual notes and community-preferred terminology. Catalogs can record contested names, explain changes over time, and identify who supplied particular descriptions. Community members should be able to propose corrections without having to prove that professional terminology has harmed them.

Digitization increases the stakes. A photograph accessible in a reading room enters a different field of risk when placed online. Search engines can detach it from its catalog record. Users can copy, alter, and redistribute it. Names and locations can become searchable. Materials originally shared within a local project may reach audiences never contemplated by their contributors.

Access decisions should therefore reflect the character of the material and the risks attached to circulation. Options can include delayed release, password protection, limited-resolution display, restrictions on reproduction, removal of personal names, community-specific access conditions, and procedures for correction or takedown. These measures require explanation and periodic review; otherwise, restrictions can become arbitrary or remain in place after their original purpose has disappeared.

The aim is to preserve access without treating circulation as ethically neutral.

 

Consent Beyond the Release Form

A signed agreement records consent at a particular moment. It cannot anticipate every future change in technology, institutional policy, political conditions, or personal circumstance. Materials that once seemed safe to share can become dangerous after migration status changes, a conflict intensifies, a government begins targeting activists, or a digital platform makes records easier to aggregate.

Ethical responsibility continues after acquisition. Participants should have opportunities to review interviews, transcripts, captions, and edited excerpts before publication. Agreements can specify whether corrections, redactions, temporary closures, or withdrawal will remain possible. Where the library cannot promise complete deletion because copies, backups, legal deposits, or third-party reuse fall beyond its control, that limit should be stated plainly.

Ongoing consent does not give every contributor unilateral control over an entire collection. Records may involve several people with conflicting rights and expectations. A testimony can belong to the narrator's experience while containing information about relatives, neighbors, alleged perpetrators, or vulnerable third parties. Removing it may protect one person while erasing evidence needed by others. Such conflicts require a documented process that considers privacy, safety, historical value, collective rights, and prior agreements.

Institutional continuity presents another difficulty. Staff change, projects end, websites migrate, and funding disappears. Ethical commitments made through personal trust can vanish when the responsible librarian leaves. Written policies, decision records, named responsibilities, and regular review help carry those commitments beyond the original relationship.

Consent should shape the continuing life of the record rather than function as a one-time administrative permission.

 

Representation Under Community Accountability

Ethical representation does not require librarians to abandon interpretation or refuse every effort to speak across differences. Community libraries depend on mediation, technical work, and encounters among people who do not share the same experience. The task is to prevent those functions from becoming unaccountable authority.

That requires clarity about who initiated a project, who made its decisions, and what contributors were able to control. It requires procedures for disagreement, correction, restriction, and refusal. It also requires librarians who can resist institutional demands for attractive stories, visible participation, unrestricted access, or rapid publication when those demands place contributors at risk.

Accountability extends beyond the moment of representation. Communities change, descriptions age, political conditions shift, and new generations dispute what earlier ones authorized. A library that presents community memory must remain answerable to those changes. Its records should reveal their conditions of production rather than claiming to speak from nowhere.

The quality of representation cannot be measured by the number of marginalized voices displayed or by the diversity of the collection. The decisive question is whether people retain meaningful authority over how their lives, knowledge, and struggles are converted into public records.

Without that authority, representation can become another form of possession: the institution speaks about the community, circulates its image, and calls the result inclusion.

Under community accountability, representation remains partial, revisable, and open to refusal. That is what prevents the library’s version of a community from replacing the community itself.
