Community-Centered Librarianship With a Decolonial Turn (13)

The Library as a Community Sanctuary

Protection, Continuity, and Institutional Restraint

 

This post is part of a series that reclaims community-centered librarianship from its institutional distortions, returning it to its roots in struggle, mutual aid, and collective survival. It treats libraries not as neutral services but as contested infrastructures shaped by power, resistance, and memory, and explores librarianship as solidarity work grounded in real communities, real conflicts, and the ongoing tension between institutional control and collective autonomy. Sanctuary Beyond Comfort

Libraries are often described as welcoming, inclusive, calm, or safe. But those qualities say relatively little about what happens when people enter them under conditions of pressure.

Someone facing eviction, migration controls, gendered violence, police attention, homelessness, or bureaucratic exclusion does not encounter the library as an abstract civic ideal. They encounter a building, a set of rules, a computer system, staff procedures, access requirements, registration forms, security practices, opening hours, and decisions about what information will be collected and retained.

A library can intensify pressures that people already face through its registration requirements, data collection, security practices, access rules, and other routine procedures. Much of that pressure is administrative. A person may already be moving through systems that demand identification, proof of residence, legal status, financial records, appointments, passwords, forms, declarations, interviews, and explanations. The library may reproduce the same demands without intending to. Access to computers may require registration, room use may depend on documentation, and digital services may generate records automatically. Staff may ask questions because procedures require them or because assistance is routinely organized around collecting information about the person being assisted.

For someone in a secure situation, these requirements may be minor. Under conditions of instability, however, their accumulation can become another layer of administrative burden and exposure. Sanctuary begins, in part, with examining what the library itself contributes to that accumulation.

 

Knowing Less

Libraries routinely collect information because collection has become embedded in ordinary administration. Registration systems request data because the software contains fields for it. Programs record attendance because funders expect participation figures. Public computers may retain histories because default settings have never been changed. Events are photographed because communications departments need images. Community initiatives are documented because institutions require evidence of impact.

None of these practices is necessarily harmful in itself. Their consequences depend on context. Information gathered as part of routine administration can become sensitive later, depending on who can access it, how long it is retained, and how the person's circumstances change.

A woman seeking information about domestic violence may not want her presence at a particular activity recorded. A migrant may hesitate to use a service if identification is required without a clear reason. A tenant facing eviction may need access to legal documents without explaining the entire conflict to staff. A young person may need to use a computer or remain in the building without having their movements made legible to adults or institutions outside the library. Someone experiencing homelessness may need a place to work through documents, applications, or communications without their presence being immediately treated as evidence of disorder.

These situations place data minimization close to the center of sanctuary. The practical questions are ordinary but consequential: what information is actually necessary, which fields can remain optional, how long records are retained, who can see them, whether sensitive conversations can remain private, whether access depends unnecessarily on proof of identity, and whether users are told clearly what happens to the information they provide.

There is a professional tendency to treat better knowledge about users as inherently beneficial. Community-centered practice complicates that assumption. Sometimes better service requires knowing more. At other times, protection depends on the institution knowing less.

 

Between Legal Obligation and Institutional Habit

Requests for information from authorities or other external actors expose another limit of sanctuary. Libraries operate within legal and administrative systems, and the protection they can offer is constrained by those systems. Depending on jurisdiction and circumstance, records may be subject to lawful requests, court orders, internal investigations, contractual arrangements, or other forms of disclosure.

A library that describes itself as sanctuary while allowing people to assume that their information can never be accessed creates a dangerous illusion. Protection requires precision about what records exist, which can be disclosed, under what authority, and through what procedures. Staff also need to know where discretion remains.

An informal request for information may carry no legal force, while a court order may require compliance. Security preferences, managerial expectations, and statutory obligations also operate at different levels of authority. When institutions collapse those differences and treat every request as compulsory, they surrender whatever room for judgment they actually possess.

That room may be limited, but it can still have material consequences. Policies governing user records, computer histories, photography, room bookings, borrowing data, referrals, or internal incident reports determine how much of a person's interaction with the institution remains available for later scrutiny. Understanding those systems should therefore be part of community-centered professional competence, particularly where users may face state scrutiny, family violence, immigration enforcement, criminalization, or other forms of exposure.

 

Continuity During Disruption

Sanctuary also has a temporal dimension. Eviction, displacement, migration, violence, and administrative crisis rarely produce a single isolated problem. They interrupt the ordinary infrastructures through which people organize daily life.

Housing loss can disrupt addresses, storage, correspondence, internet access, access to documents, relationships with neighbors, proximity to schools and workplaces, and the ability to keep track of administrative processes. Migration can fracture linguistic continuity, social networks, knowledge of public institutions, access to records, and communication with people elsewhere. Gendered violence may force someone to leave a home, change devices, abandon accounts, avoid familiar places, or stop using ordinary communication channels.

In these situations, the library may become valuable because it remains relatively stable while other infrastructures disappear. It may provide access to court records, municipal information, legal databases, printers, scanners, telephones, internet connections, translation tools, local orientation, meeting rooms, or organizations capable of providing specialized assistance. It may also offer something less visible: continuity across different stages of a disrupted situation.

A person facing eviction may return several times while trying to reconstruct a timeline, locate documents, contact organizations, submit forms, or understand an administrative process. A migrant may use the same place repeatedly while rebuilding an informational environment around unfamiliar institutions. A neighborhood organization that loses its meeting space may continue working through the library. Documents can be copied, communications maintained, sources revisited, and unfinished work resumed.

This kind of continuity is easily underestimated because it is mundane. A working computer, a scanner, predictable opening hours, a table, a telephone, or a staff member who knows where to locate a municipal record may appear trivial in isolation. Together, they can preserve a thread between one stage of a disrupted life and the next.

 

Protection Without Capture

Libraries working with people under pressure often accumulate compelling stories. Those stories can easily become part of the institution's own narrative: the migrant helped by the library, the survivor who found information there, the neighborhood group that organized in its meeting room, the displaced family that used its services, or the tenant campaign that consulted its collections.

Such accounts can demonstrate public value, attract funding, justify programs, support advocacy, and produce powerful professional communication. However, they can also transform other people's vulnerability into institutional capital.

The pressure to document success is especially strong in community work. Funders expect outcomes. Administrators want evidence. Communications departments want stories. Professional conferences seek examples. Libraries themselves may want to demonstrate that their work has consequences beyond traditional circulation statistics.

Under those conditions, assistance can gradually acquire an expectation of visibility. A person who came to solve an immediate problem may later be invited to explain their experience, appear in photographs, contribute testimony, or authorize the use of their story in institutional reporting. Even where participation is technically voluntary, the power relationship can make refusal difficult.

Sanctuary requires a sharper separation between access to help and participation in institutional storytelling. People do not acquire an obligation to become evidence of a library's success because they used its resources during a difficult period. The same applies to organized groups. A tenants' association using a meeting room does not automatically become a library program. A migrant network meeting there does not become evidence of outreach. A group documenting police abuse does not become institutional content because staff helped locate records or preserve files.

Maintaining that separation requires tolerance for activity that produces no obvious return to the institution.

 

When Nothing Measurable Happens

Contemporary management systems increasingly demand evidence of impact. Community work is therefore pushed toward forms that can be counted, photographed, summarized, evaluated, and reported. Sanctuary often sits awkwardly within those systems because many of its most important effects are difficult to capture.

A quiet conversation, an avoided disclosure, an undocumented meeting, the decision not to collect unnecessary data, or the ability of someone to spend several hours resolving a problem without being disturbed may produce little that can be entered into a dashboard. The administrative record may show almost nothing, even where the person's situation has been materially improved.

This tension creates a familiar problem. Institutions tend to privilege activities whose value can be demonstrated through participation numbers, outputs, public visibility, or measurable outcomes. Protective restraint may look like inactivity because its success consists partly in what did not occur: information was not collected, exposure was avoided, a person was not expelled, an unnecessary barrier was not imposed, or a group was allowed to work without being absorbed into an institutional program.

Community-centered evaluation therefore has to leave room for forms of value that resist easy conversion into evidence. Otherwise, sanctuary practices are gradually replaced by activities that are easier to document and justify.

 

Conflicting Claims to Protection

Sanctuary cannot be understood only from the perspective of the person seeking protection. Libraries are shared environments, and the interests present within them can conflict. Confidentiality may protect one person while concealing information relevant to another. Restrictions on access may prevent harmful exposure while also limiting legitimate scrutiny. Informality may reduce bureaucratic barriers while weakening safeguards designed to protect vulnerable users or workers.

The same space may also feel radically different to different people. Measures introduced to protect one group may increase anxiety for another. Security practices intended to prevent violence can make racialized, undocumented, homeless, or young users feel watched or unwelcome. Conversely, removing every form of intervention may leave other users or staff exposed to harassment or physical danger.

There is no single configuration that produces sanctuary for everyone under all conditions. Libraries have to work through competing claims, unequal risks, legal obligations, and the consequences of their own decisions. That work becomes more credible when conflicts are acknowledged rather than hidden behind generalized claims of safety and inclusion.

Community authority remains important here, but it cannot resolve every contradiction automatically. Communities contain internal differences, unequal power, conflicting interests, and competing interpretations of what protection requires. Decisions about confidentiality, access, visibility, and risk therefore require continuing negotiation rather than a fixed formula.

 

Protecting the Protectors

Library workers themselves inhabit these conflicts. A librarian asked to protect confidentiality may face institutional pressure. A worker expected to intervene in a violent situation may have neither the training nor the protection required to do so. Staff employed through temporary contracts may possess little authority to challenge procedures. Security personnel may operate under policies written elsewhere. Frontline workers may also face harassment, threats, emotional exhaustion, or disciplinary consequences when they exercise discretion.

Expectations that the library provide sanctuary can easily become another moral burden imposed on workers whose own conditions are precarious. An institution cannot credibly build protection for users by transferring unlimited responsibility to individuals at the service desk.

Institutional restraint therefore needs organizational support. Policies must be clear enough that workers know what discretion they possess. Training should address privacy, referral, de-escalation, legal requests, documentation, and the limits of professional responsibility. Staff need mechanisms for escalating situations that exceed their competence or authority, and workers who challenge harmful procedures need protection from retaliation where possible.

Otherwise, sanctuary depends on personal courage. That may produce admirable individual acts, but it does not produce a reliable institutional practice.

 

The Limits of Sanctuary

Libraries cannot remove people from every form of state power, stop violence outside their buildings, resolve housing crises, dismantle migration regimes, or substitute for specialized legal, medical, social, or emergency services. Their resources, authority, staffing, and legal capacities are limited.

Those limits should be explicit.

The danger lies in turning sanctuary into institutional mythology and allowing the language of refuge to obscure what a library can and cannot actually guarantee. If a person needs emergency housing, physical protection, legal representation, medical care, or specialist support, a library may help identify the appropriate service or provide the informational conditions for reaching it, but the distinction between those roles should remain clear.

The more useful question concerns the part of the situation that does fall within library control. Libraries can examine how aggressively they collect information, whether identification is genuinely necessary, how user records are retained, how sensitive conversations are handled, how requests for information are evaluated, how vulnerability is represented publicly, and how access systems affect people already facing instability.

They can also provide continuity of information, communication, documentation, and space during periods when other parts of a person's life are being disrupted. These decisions do not eliminate the larger structures producing harm, but they determine whether the library reproduces those pressures inside its own institutional territory.

 

Reducing Institutional Pressure

Coercive systems rarely operate through one dramatic act. They accumulate through procedures: another form, another record, another demand for identification, another requirement to explain, another institution gathering information, another place where presence becomes conditional.

A community-centered library occupies only a small part of that landscape, but within that part it possesses real choices. It can multiply those demands, ignore them as ordinary bureaucracy, or examine whether they are actually necessary. It can turn vulnerability into data and publicity, or limit what it collects and claims. It can allow disruption to produce complete informational fragmentation, or provide enough continuity for people to keep working through whatever has happened to them.

Seen from this perspective, sanctuary does not depend on presenting the library as a refuge outside society. The library remains implicated in the same legal, administrative, technological, and political systems operating elsewhere.

The library's responsibility begins with the part of this landscape it can actually control: its own procedures, records, access rules, and decisions about exposure. Sanctuary takes shape in those ordinary institutional choices, and in the extent to which they reduce the pressures already acting on the people who enter.
