Indigenous Knowledge and Memory (02)

Indigenous Records Without Their Histories

Provenance, Context, and the Afterlives of Colonial Collections

 

This post is part of a series exploring the relationships between Indigenous knowledge systems, libraries, and archives. It examines the histories, practices, conflicts, and responsibilities involved in documenting, preserving, organizing, and transmitting Indigenous knowledge, with particular attention to cultural authority and the limits of institutional intervention. The Wrong Photograph

In the mid-1990s, a historical photograph appeared on the programme of the Woodford Folk Festival in Queensland, Australia. It supposedly showed one of the last great Aboriginal gatherings associated with the traditional Bunya Festival, held in the nineteenth century.

The image was convincing. A group of Aboriginal people stood together outdoors, apparently participating in an event that belonged to the region's Indigenous history. For readers unfamiliar with the photograph, there was little reason to question its identification.

Except that the photograph showed something entirely different.

It had been taken on 12 November 1892 at Chelmer Reach, on the Brisbane River, during a picnic organized by the Queensland Press Club. The people appearing in it belonged to the Wild Australia Show, a troupe of Aboriginal performers who toured Australian cities during the early 1890s.

The mistake had not originated with the festival organizers. The photograph came from the State Library of Queensland, where it had been incorrectly associated with the Noosa district. Somewhere in its documentary history, the image had become separated from the event it actually represented.

The incorrect identification continued to circulate. The photograph appeared in local historical publications and in research and reports related to Aboriginal native title claims, where it was used as evidence of a gathering it had never depicted. The confusion was eventually reconstructed through research by Michael Aird, Paul Memmott, and their collaborators. By examining historical newspapers, photographs, and other records, they established the circumstances in which the image had been produced.

What makes this case interesting is not simply that an archivist or a researcher made a mistake. Errors are inevitable in historical work. The important point is what happened afterward.

An incorrect institutional description became the basis for subsequent interpretations. The photograph acquired a new historical identity, which researchers reproduced without necessarily examining the evidence supporting it.

The archive had preserved the image. It had also helped preserve an inaccurate history of that image.

 

When the Catalogue Becomes the Evidence

Collections do not remain unchanged after entering a library or archive. They are arranged, described, classified, copied, exhibited, and incorporated into new systems of access. These activities generate information that future researchers use to understand the materials.

A catalogue may identify a manuscript's author, a photograph's location, or an object's cultural origin. Ideally, such information derives from reliable historical evidence. Sometimes, however, it comes from an uncertain annotation, an earlier catalogue, or an inference made by someone who had limited knowledge of the material. Over time, the distinction between what the original record establishes and what a later cataloguer believed about it can become difficult to recognize.

Consider an unidentified photograph assigned to a particular location because its scenery resembles other images from that area. The attribution enters the catalogue. A historian subsequently uses the photograph in a publication, citing the institutional description. Another researcher reproduces the image and cites the historian. Eventually, several publications may agree on the photograph's location, although all depend on the same original guess. Repetition has created the appearance of independent confirmation.

This process is especially consequential for Indigenous materials because many institutional descriptions were produced by people whose knowledge of the societies they documented was incomplete. Names, geographical attributions, linguistic identifications, and explanations of practices could reflect the collector's understanding rather than that of the people represented. Some descriptions were carefully researched. Others were provisional or mistaken. Their reliability cannot be established simply by observing that they appear in an institutional catalogue.

The problem becomes more serious when the original context is no longer available for comparison. An institutional description can then become the principal source of information about a document, even though it was originally intended only to help people locate and identify that document.

In such circumstances, the history assigned to a record may gradually replace the history of the record itself.

 

The Performers Who Became Anonymous

The Wild Australia Show provides another illustration of what this transformation can mean.

The troupe consisted of twenty-seven Aboriginal performers recruited from northern Queensland. During 1892 and 1893, they travelled and performed in several Australian cities, appearing before audiences interested in exhibitions of Indigenous cultures.

Commercial photographers produced portraits of the performers. Some photographs were intended for publicity; others circulated as studio images or postcards.

These photographs survived, but many eventually lost their original identifications. An image of four performers was described as Group of Black Trackers, concealing the fact that the men were members of a professional touring troupe when the photograph was taken.

A portrait of Dangakura, a Wakaya man who participated in the show, circulated under the generic label An Aboriginal. A photograph of an anonymous Aboriginal man may be interpreted as an ethnographic representation of an Indigenous population. The same photograph, identified as a portrait of Dangakura, opens a different historical investigation.

Who was he? How did he become involved in the troupe? Where did he travel? What work did he perform? How did commercial photography represent him, and what happened to his image afterward? The generic identification removes those questions from view.

This is one reason the research conducted by Aird and Memmott was significant. By reconnecting photographs with contemporary newspapers and other sources, they recovered the identities of performers and reconstructed aspects of their experiences. The research supported the exhibition Wild Australia, presented at the University of Queensland Anthropology Museum in 2015.

It also allowed other institutions to reconsider their holdings. At the University of Sydney's Macleay Museum, curator Rebecca Conway identified additional photographs of the troupe, including images of thirteen people who could now be named.

The discovery did more than correct some catalogue entries. It changed what the photographs documented. Images that had been interpreted primarily as generic representations of Aboriginal people could now be examined as records of particular individuals, occupations, journeys, and encounters with colonial society.

The photographs had not acquired new content. Researchers had recovered relationships between the images and their historical circumstances.

This raises a question about provenance. An institution may know who donated a photograph, who previously owned it, and when it entered a collection. That information is valuable. But does it tell us enough about the photograph itself?

The history of its institutional custody and the history of the people it depicts are not necessarily the same. And sometimes the former survives precisely because nobody thought to record the latter.

 

A Maya Manuscript with an Unknown Past

Missing provenance does not always produce an incorrect identification. Sometimes it creates uncertainty that researchers cannot easily resolve.

The Códice Maya de México, formerly known as the Grolier Codex, offers a different example.

The manuscript appeared in Mexico during the 1960s, reportedly discovered under circumstances that were never reliably documented. No controlled archaeological excavation established where it had been found or how it had passed into private hands.

In 1971, it was exhibited at the Grolier Club in New York. Its appearance provoked a prolonged scholarly controversy. Was it an authentic pre-Hispanic Maya manuscript, or a modern forgery?

The question was difficult because its unusual style and content did not correspond neatly to the other surviving Maya codices. The absence of reliable archaeological provenance made the problem worse.

Had the manuscript come from a documented excavation, researchers might have been able to examine its relationship to other objects, the archaeological site, and independently dated materials. Instead, much of that context was unavailable.

Some specialists defended its authenticity. Others questioned it. During the following decades, researchers studied its material composition, paintings, astronomical content, and iconography. A major reassessment by Michael Coe, Stephen Houston, Mary Miller, and Karl Taube in 2016 supported its identification as a genuine pre-Hispanic manuscript.

Further interdisciplinary investigations organized by Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History confirmed its authenticity. In 2018, Mexican authorities formally recognized the manuscript as the Códice Maya de México.

Research places its production between the eleventh and twelfth centuries. It is now regarded as the oldest surviving readable book from the Americas. Yet the circumstances of its modern discovery remain incompletely documented.

The manuscript's authenticity could be established through scientific and historical investigation without reconstructing every stage of its provenance.

The missing information still matters. It limits what can be said about the manuscript's original archaeological setting and later circulation. But it does not prevent researchers from establishing other facts.

The case reminds us that provenance is not a single question with a single answer. We may know when a document was created without knowing where it was discovered. We may reconstruct its institutional history without understanding how it left its original setting. And we may possess reliable evidence about some aspects of its history while remaining uncertain about others.

 

Can We Recover a Missing History?

In some circumstances, the missing connections can be reconstructed.

The Wild Australia Show photographs could be identified because contemporary newspapers, other photographs, and historical documentation had survived. Researchers were able to compare these sources and recognize people and events that previous descriptions had obscured.

The Maya codex presented a different problem. Its archaeological context could not be recovered, but scientific analysis established important facts about its production.

Other collections offer fewer possibilities.

An object may have been purchased without any information about its maker. A photograph may have been taken without recording the names of those depicted. A collector may have documented an acquisition without investigating the earlier history of the material.

Sometimes the relevant information existed but was subsequently lost. Sometimes it was never recorded. A gap in an institutional catalogue does not necessarily mean that the corresponding knowledge has disappeared. Names, places, and historical relationships may remain known to families or communities, or be documented in sources that the institution has never consulted.

Equally, we cannot assume that every missing detail can eventually be recovered. Historical research must distinguish between what is established, what is probable, what remains disputed, and what cannot presently be known.

There is another complication. When a description is corrected, the earlier interpretation does not necessarily become irrelevant. The mistaken identification of the Bunya Festival photograph is itself part of the photograph's later history. It explains why the image circulated in certain publications and how it became associated with an event it did not depict. The old description should no longer be presented as fact, but its existence remains historically significant.

This means that a record can have several successive documentary identities. Some are supported by evidence; others reflect errors, assumptions, or changing interpretations. Reconstructing its history involves understanding those changes rather than simply replacing an old caption with a new one.

 

The History We Think We Know

The materials preserved in libraries and archives have histories that extend beyond their physical survival.

A photograph can be removed from its original setting, acquire an incorrect description, appear in publications, and eventually become evidence for an event it never documented. A portrait can lose the name of its subject and become a generic ethnographic illustration. A manuscript can survive for centuries while essential information about its discovery or circulation remains unknown.

These are different problems, but they reveal something important about documentary institutions. Libraries and archives preserve evidence. They also produce information about that evidence, and their descriptions influence how subsequent generations understand the past.

Historical materials require interpretation, and much of what we know about them depends on careful institutional research. The difficulty arises when interpretations acquire the appearance of established facts without retaining the evidence, assumptions, and uncertainties on which they were based.

For Indigenous collections, the consequences can be particularly significant because the surviving institutional records frequently privilege the perspectives of collectors, administrators, and researchers. Those histories may be accurately documented within their own limits, but they do not necessarily account for the people, purposes, and relationships associated with the materials before or beyond their institutional custody.

The history preserved by an institution and the history of the material it preserves are not necessarily the same. Both can be valuable, and both require investigation. An acquisition record may accurately document a transaction while revealing little about a document's earlier life. A catalogue may faithfully reproduce an old attribution without establishing whether it was ever correct.

The consequences become visible when those descriptions circulate beyond the institution. Researchers cite them, publications reproduce them, and historical interpretations begin to depend on information whose origins may no longer be clear. The history of the Wild Australia Show photographs demonstrates how far an incorrect identification can travel, and how much research may be needed to establish what the original record actually represents.

Preserving a document also means preserving the evidence needed to understand it. That evidence includes the history of the descriptions and interpretations attached to the document over time, including those that later prove mistaken. Without it, future researchers may inherit not only the records themselves, but also assumptions about their past that have acquired the appearance of historical fact.
