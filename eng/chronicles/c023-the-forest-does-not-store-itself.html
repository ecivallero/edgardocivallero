Biomimesis in Action (04)

The Forest Does Not Store Itself

Persistence through regeneration

 

After Dormancy, Dispersion, and Inhibition

The first three posts in this series examined forms of persistence that depend on reduced activity. Resurrection plants suspend ordinary function while preserving the conditions of return. Desert seed banks distribute possibility across time, so that survival is not committed to a single season. Peatlands slow decomposition by altering the environment in which decay can proceed.

These mechanisms are useful for libraries, archives, museums, repositories, and community memory projects because they unsettle a familiar institutional expectation: that continuity should appear as constant activity, full visibility, stable custody, or uninterrupted access. They show how fragile knowledge systems may survive through latency, dispersed viability, delayed activation, or carefully limited exposure.

The forest introduces another problem.

Here, persistence is organized through replacement. A forest continues while its material components pass away, return, decompose, feed, shade, open, and are succeeded by others. Its endurance does not depend on keeping previous forms intact. It depends on the renewal of relations that allow the system to keep producing forest conditions.

For memory institutions, this shift are relevant because storage can protect materials while the capacities around them weaken. A collection may remain physically secure while its descriptive logic becomes obscure. Custodial memory may become personal, then disappear with retirement, death, conflict, or institutional change. Technical systems may be migrated while their older structure loses legibility. Communities connected to materials may remain named in records while actual relations with them deteriorate.

The forest gives this problem a precise form. It moves the discussion from persistence as retention to persistence as regeneration. It asks how libraries, archives, and museums can sustain continuity when staff, catalogues, classifications, platforms, descriptions, ethical standards, and interpretive frameworks inevitably change. It also asks how renewal can occur without turning previous layers into institutional sediment that nobody can read.

Stored collections can outlive the systems that know how to care for them. The forest helps examine that danger.

 

Continuity Made Through Replacement

Seen from a distance, a forest may give the impression of stability: a persistent boundary on a hill, a canopy that continues to mark a place, a name that outlives the organisms composing it. Yet this apparent continuity depends on material turnover. Leaves are renewed, branches fall, roots die into the ground they helped structure, openings in the canopy alter light and moisture, and young plants establish themselves within conditions produced by older growth.

This kind of continuity is neither simple stability nor simple change, because the forest remains through replacement organized by inheritance. Dead wood, shade, disturbance, soil, moisture, and altered light do not act as separate illustrations of continuity; together they form the conditions through which earlier growth continues to matter after it has ceased to exist as living structure. What survives, then, is not the earlier forest as a preserved object, but its capacity to shape the field in which later growth becomes possible.

This is the mechanism that matters for biomimicry in knowledge institutions. Forest regeneration does not tell libraries, archives, and museums to become more "natural." It gives a way to think about systems whose persistence depends on the renewal of function across material and organizational turnover.

In preservation language, the retained item often dominates attention. The manuscript, object, specimen, recording, photograph, dataset, or book appears as the primary bearer of continuity. The activities around it can then seem secondary: cataloguing, rights management, provenance research, migration, consultation, reinterpretation, conservation notes, access protocols, and staff training. Regeneration reverses the emphasis. The item cannot remain institutionally alive unless those surrounding capacities continue to be renewed.

A sound recording may remain playable while losing the contextual conditions that make listening responsible. A museum object may remain conserved while its provenance hardens into an inherited uncertainty nobody is assigned to investigate. A catalogue record may remain searchable while the rationale behind its terms has vanished. Preservation may have succeeded at the level of matter — and failed at the level of relation.

Forest regeneration makes that failure easier to name. It suggests that the persistence of a knowledge system depends on more than retained holdings: it depends on the repeated production of conditions through which holdings can still be described, questioned, connected, restricted, interpreted, and transmitted.

 

The Stored Collection and the Exhausted Institution

Memory institutions can become exhausted around collections they continue to hold. This exhaustion rarely appears as dramatic collapse. It accumulates quietly when decisions stop being explained, descriptive habits continue after their reasons have disappeared, technical migrations preserve content while flattening structure, or access rules survive as policy language without living custodial relation behind them.

The collection may still be safe in the sense most visible to institutional reporting. It has been accessioned, housed, insured, digitized, or entered into a database. Yet the deeper preservation question concerns whether the institution can still regenerate understanding around it. Who can explain why it was arranged this way? Who can interpret its silences? Who knows which terms were local, which were imposed, which were provisional, and which should no longer govern description? Who can distinguish a restriction grounded in ethical obligation from one inherited through bureaucratic caution?

These questions belong to preservation because they decide whether stored materials can remain knowledge-bearing. A library, archive, or museum does not merely retain things: it sustains systems of relation around them. When those systems are allowed to thin, the holdings remain while their interpretive environment becomes less capable.

Forest regeneration provides a useful analogy here because it treats continuity as an active field rather than a static container. A forest does not carry its previous form forward by locking it away. Earlier growth remains effective through altered soil, changed light, microbial activity, root space, seedling recruitment, and accumulated organic matter. The previous forest participates in the future forest by shaping the conditions in which replacement happens.

Knowledge institutions require comparable forms of inheritance. Revisions, migrations, corrections, and transfers should not erase the conditions that made them necessary or intelligible. The reasons behind a revised catalogue, the structure of a migrated platform, the history of corrected terms, and the path through which custody changed all belong to the continuity of the system. Without that recoverable layer, renewal becomes a technical or administrative event rather than a preserved relation between past decisions, present responsibilities, and future interpretation.

Regeneration therefore requires a particular discipline. It allows replacement, but it does not allow replacement to behave as a clean beginning.

 

Succession as Preservation Work

Succession is one of the most neglected forms of preservation. Institutions often treat it as an administrative matter, associated with staffing, handover, or continuity planning. In practice, succession determines whether a knowledge system can survive the disappearance of the people who carried its unwritten structure.

Libraries, archives, and museums depend on tacit knowledge. Some of it is technical, some descriptive, some ethical, some relational. A person may know why a group of materials was kept apart from a formal series, why a donor's terminology was retained with caution, why a particular community contact should be approached before access is granted, or why a metadata field contains irregular values. Such knowledge may never appear in the public record. It may not even appear in internal documentation. Yet without it, future workers inherit decisions without inheriting their conditions.

Forest regeneration depends on temporal overlap. Seedlings develop before the canopy opens fully. Young growth waits under the influence of older growth. Dead matter begins transforming the ground before later organisms use it. The future does not begin after the present has vanished; it is prepared inside it.

Memory institutions need this overlap. Succession should occur before crisis. Documentation should be written while the people who understand the system can still explain it. Descriptive rationales should be recorded before categories become habit. Technical notes should accompany migration before fields lose their history. Community relations should not be stored only in the memory of one curator, archivist, librarian, project founder, or volunteer.

In fragile knowledge systems, this becomes urgent. Community archives, small museums, independent repositories, and temporary research projects often depend on intense personal labor. Their continuity may rest on trust, improvisation, and local knowledge that formal institutions rarely see. When such systems are transferred, digitized, professionalized, or absorbed, succession can easily become possession without inheritance. Materials move, while the conditions that made them meaningful remain behind.

A regenerative approach would treat succession as part of the collection's preservation environment. It would ask how responsibility, context, uncertainty, and restriction can pass across time without being reduced to ownership. It would treat a handover note, a decision log, a data dictionary, a provenance uncertainty, or a recorded access rationale as preservation tissue. These forms do not merely support the collection. They help the collection remain capable of future interpretation.

 

Regeneration Without Erasure

Regeneration carries risk because renewal can hide what it replaces. A forest may return after disturbance, yet the returned forest may differ in composition, density, rhythm, and ecological relation. The fact that a forest remains does not mean that all previous relations continued. Persistence at one scale can conceal loss at another.

Libraries, archives, and museums face a similar danger whenever repair or modernization alters the systems through which knowledge has been organized. Improved access, corrected terminology, reclassification, and data migration can all be necessary, yet each can also conceal the older structures it replaces. Authority may have been organized through records that are later overwritten; damaging language may disappear without leaving evidence of its former legitimacy; corrected arrangements may make previous intellectual pathways impossible to reconstruct; and technical upgrades may discard fields whose local meaning was greater than their apparent administrative value.

These cases show why regeneration must preserve evidence of transformation. Older descriptions may need to lose authority, especially when they reproduce colonial, racial, disciplinary, or administrative violence. Yet their disappearance can also protect the institution from accountability. If previous terms, categories, omissions, and misattributions vanish without trace, future users cannot study how the institution produced knowledge before it corrected itself.

The aim is to prevent harmful language from continuing to govern description while still preserving evidence of how that language once operated. Institutional change needs layered records in which deprecated terms, superseded classifications, corrected attributions, and revised access policies remain traceable according to their evidential value and ethical risk. Such layers do not grant equal authority to every previous form; they allow the institution to show what changed, why it changed, and how renewal can proceed without converting correction into amnesia.

This matters especially in reparative description, provenance research, decolonial cataloguing, and museum reinterpretation. These practices require change, but they also require memory of the conditions being changed. Regeneration becomes serious when it can transform the present record while preserving the evidence of why transformation was necessary.

The forest is useful here because it refuses pure replacement. New growth happens in altered ground. Earlier forms continue through effects, not as intact originals. In knowledge institutions, earlier forms also persist through effects: in naming conventions, acquisition histories, classification habits, access assumptions, and patterns of absence. Responsible regeneration makes those effects available for scrutiny.

 

When Renewal Becomes Displacement

The language of renewal is easy to corrupt because it can make institutional disruption appear generous, progressive, or inevitable. Terms such as modernization, sustainability, innovation, and integration often name necessary work, but they can also conceal the redistribution of control, labor, and meaning: authority becomes centralized in the name of modernization; maintenance is pushed onto weaker actors in the name of sustainability; situated vocabularies are displaced in the name of innovation; and collections are absorbed into frameworks that improve management while reducing interpretive depth.

Biomimicry becomes dangerous when it naturalizes these operations. Forests do not provide moral permission for memory institutions to damage knowledge systems and then admire whatever survives. Ecological regeneration should not be used to justify austerity, forced migration, institutional withdrawal, unpaid care, or the replacement of situated knowledge by administratively convenient order.

The danger is real because regeneration carries an attractive moral charge: it suggests vitality, repair, and future possibility. In libraries, archives, and museums, however, renewal becomes displacement when replacement breaks the relations that gave a system its accountability. Standard vocabularies can weaken the conceptual force of local classifications; larger repositories can offer storage while reducing community control over access; new interpretive frameworks can reorganize collections while neglecting prior custodial obligations; and sustainability discourse can disguise the transfer of maintenance onto precarious workers. In such cases, the institution may appear renewed while the relations that made continuity meaningful have been severed.

Such changes may produce continuity of possession, visibility, or institutional reporting. They do not necessarily produce continuity of relation.

Regeneration therefore requires governance. Replacement must be documented, resourced, and made answerable to those whose relations to the materials are affected by it; otherwise, renewal becomes a technical event with ethical consequences nobody has agreed to carry. Description, custody, platform migration, and access all alter the conditions under which a collection remains intelligible, so each must preserve the rationale, obligations, structures, and ethical limits that make continuity accountable.

The forest does not solve these problems. It sharpens them. It shows that replacement can sustain a system, while also reminding us that replacement can alter the system so thoroughly that continuity becomes a word covering loss.

 

The Forest Does Not Store Itself

The forest persists by reproducing the conditions of continuation after particular forms disappear. Succession, decomposition, disturbance, recruitment, and inheritance do not preserve an earlier forest intact; they allow later growth to remain related to what preceded it.

For libraries, archives, and museums, this mechanism exposes the limit of storage as a total preservation model. Storage is indispensable, but it can also leave behind a dead continuity: materials retained after the descriptive, custodial, technical, and ethical conditions that made them meaningful have weakened or failed. Regeneration adds a second demand. What has been preserved must remain capable of entering renewed structures of interpretation, responsibility, and use without being detached from the histories that shaped it.

This is the value of the forest for biomimicry in memory institutions. It identifies a layer of preservation concerned not with holding materials alone, but with renewing the capacities through which held materials remain intelligible and accountable across changing staff, systems, categories, policies, and communities.

The forest does not store itself. It continues because its conditions of continuation are reproduced through change. Libraries, archives, and museums face a comparable boundary: they cannot preserve knowledge simply by holding its remains. They must regenerate the arrangements through which those remains can still answer, trouble, instruct, and belong.
