Biomimesis in Action (12)

Life Without Sunlight

Hydrothermal vent ecosystems

 

An unexpected abundance

In 1977, scientists exploring the Galápagos Rift descended approximately 2,500 meters beneath the surface of the Pacific Ocean. They were investigating submarine thermal springs, places where seawater circulating through the oceanic crust returns to the seafloor carrying heat and dissolved chemical compounds. What they encountered around those springs was extraordinary: dense communities of animals living in permanent darkness, including large clams and other organisms whose abundance seemed difficult to reconcile with their surroundings (Corliss et al. 1979).

Most life on the deep ocean floor depends, directly or indirectly, on organic material produced in the illuminated waters above it. Dead organisms, fragments of tissues, fecal pellets, and other particles descend through the water column, providing nourishment to communities far below the reach of sunlight. Only a fraction of the organic matter produced near the surface reaches the deep seafloor.

The abundance around hydrothermal vents required another explanation. Something was producing organic matter locally, in an environment where photosynthesis was impossible.

The discovery challenged a fundamental assumption about the organization of ecosystems. Sunlight, the energy source sustaining most biological productivity on Earth, could be absent from the immediate environment without preventing the development of a complex biological community.

For libraries, archives, and other knowledge infrastructures operating under precarious conditions, that discovery opens an interesting line of inquiry. When a system loses access to the resources that ordinarily sustain its activity, could it reorganize around a fundamentally different source of support?

 

Another way of making a living

Hydrothermal circulation begins when seawater penetrates fractures in the oceanic crust. As it moves through heated rock, its chemical composition changes. It can acquire dissolved minerals and reduced chemical compounds, eventually returning to the ocean through openings in the seafloor.

The biological importance of this process becomes particularly apparent where hydrothermal fluids encounter surrounding seawater. The two fluids have different chemical properties, and their interaction creates conditions from which certain microorganisms can obtain energy.

Chemosynthetic bacteria and archaea exploit these chemical differences. Depending on the organism and the local environment, compounds such as hydrogen sulfide or molecular hydrogen can participate in reactions that provide the energy required to convert inorganic carbon into organic matter. This production supports food webs that would be impossible if their development depended entirely on organic material descending from the ocean surface (Dick 2019).

Hydrothermal vents therefore sustain biological productivity through an alternative metabolic pathway. Heat contributes to creating the geological and chemical conditions, but it is the energy available through chemical reactions that many of these microorganisms actually exploit.

There is a considerable difference between discovering another source of energy and developing the biological machinery capable of making that energy useful. A chemical compound that provides nourishment to one organism may be toxic or metabolically inaccessible to another.

The resources present in an environment cannot be understood independently of the capacities of its inhabitants.

 

An animal that does not eat

Among the inhabitants of Pacific hydrothermal vents is the giant tubeworm Riftia pachyptila, an animal whose anatomy illustrates how profoundly an alternative source of nourishment can transform an organism.

Adult specimens have neither a mouth nor a digestive tract. Instead, a substantial part of their body is occupied by an organ called the trophosome, which houses symbiotic bacteria. The worm supplies these microorganisms with the substances required for their metabolism, while the bacteria produce the organic compounds upon which the animal depends for nutrition.

Research published in 1981 established the foundations for understanding this extraordinary association (Cavanaugh et al. 1981). Subsequent studies have revealed the physiological complexity of the relationship, including specialized hemoglobins capable of transporting both oxygen and sulfide to the bacterial symbionts.

The association has produced an animal organized around a different nutritional economy. Its anatomy, physiology, and relationship with its surroundings have developed accordingly.

This is perhaps the most interesting aspect of hydrothermal vent ecosystems for the design of knowledge infrastructures. Alternative resources may become genuinely useful only when the structures through which they are incorporated undergo substantial transformation.

An institution designed around one particular source of support may be poorly equipped to benefit from another.

 

The institutional metabolism of knowledge

Libraries and archives have their own resource economies. Collections require physical space, electricity, equipment, technical expertise, descriptive labor, conservation materials, legal assistance, and mechanisms for transferring responsibility. These resources are usually assembled through institutional budgets, professional employment, public funding, and established administrative arrangements.

Those arrangements shape almost everything the institution does, from the selection of software to the frequency with which records are revised.

When funding disappears, the usual response is to seek its replacement. An institution applies for another grant, reduces expenditure, postpones activities, or attempts to attract a new sponsor. The underlying organizational structure generally remains oriented toward the same kind of resource supply.

For many small documentary initiatives, particularly those operating outside established institutions, that supply may never become available.

Consider a local archive documenting agricultural practices, oral histories, and environmental changes in a rural region. A conventional institutional model would require premises, salaried personnel, recording equipment, a digital repository, and a budget for fieldwork and maintenance. Without those resources, the archive may remain indefinitely at the proposal stage.

Yet the region already contains activities through which documentary material is generated. Farmers exchange information about crops and weather. Teachers prepare educational materials. Families maintain photographs and correspondence. Local organizations produce maps, meeting records, and accounts of environmental conflicts. Community radio stations collect voices and testimonies. These activities possess documentary potential, although they were rarely organized with archival purposes in mind.

A different infrastructure could emerge around them. Agreements concerning custody, recording protocols, shared descriptive practices, permissions, and the exchange of copies could allow existing activities to contribute to a collective documentary system. Such an archive would require a particular kind of professional work. Its development would involve identifying where documentary production already occurs, establishing relationships with those responsible for it, and designing mechanisms through which materials can acquire continuity, context, and recoverability.

Its architecture would follow the distribution of available resources rather than reproduce the structure of an institution whose resources are absent.

 

Where different currents meet

The productivity of hydrothermal vents depends substantially on the encounter between chemically different environments. Vent fluids provide certain compounds, while surrounding seawater supplies others. Their interaction creates opportunities for metabolism within particular physical and chemical ranges.

This suggests a further possibility for knowledge systems. Some of their most valuable capacities may develop at the intersection of activities that were previously unrelated.

A school possesses equipment and an established rhythm of educational work. A community organization maintains relationships with local inhabitants. A university has technical expertise and computing infrastructure. A radio station possesses recording experience and access to oral testimony. Individually, none of them may be capable of establishing and maintaining an archive. Their combination, however, could support documentary functions that no participant could sustain independently.

The difficulty lies in designing the interfaces between them. Recordings must remain associated with their contexts of production. Descriptions must be understandable beyond the organization that created them. Responsibilities for preservation must be assigned. Technical arrangements must accommodate differences in equipment and expertise. Agreements must establish who can authorize access, reproduction, or withdrawal.

These interfaces constitute infrastructure in their own right. They allow resources produced within one activity to become useful within another without necessarily transferring ownership or placing every participant under a common institutional authority.

For the librarian or archivist, this opens a field of intervention that extends beyond the administration of collections. It involves creating the conditions through which otherwise disconnected resources can sustain documentary activity.

 

The darkness has its dependencies

There is an important qualification to the apparent independence of hydrothermal vent ecosystems.

Although their primary production does not require local sunlight, many vent organisms depend on oxygen dissolved in seawater, ultimately supplied by photosynthesis elsewhere on the planet. The giant tubeworm itself participates in this wider relationship. Its bacterial partners exploit chemical energy originating in hydrothermal processes, but oxygen remains essential to their metabolism.

The darkness of the deep ocean is therefore connected to the illuminated surface. Alternative productivity does not imply complete ecological independence.

This qualification is particularly relevant when considering autonomous or community-based knowledge infrastructures. An archive may operate without conventional institutional funding while remaining dependent on telecommunications networks, manufactured storage devices, public education, commercial software, or expertise acquired through established institutions. Some of those dependencies are readily visible. Others become apparent only when they fail.

There is also a political danger in celebrating the capacity of fragile systems to survive with alternative resources. An institution's withdrawal of financial support cannot automatically be justified by the ability of communities to compensate through unpaid labor. Nor should the ingenuity of documentary initiatives operating under difficult conditions become an argument against providing them with adequate resources.

Identifying alternative sources of support should expand the possibilities available to knowledge workers and communities. It should not absolve institutions of their responsibilities.

 

Beyond the search for another sun

A documentary project confronting the disappearance of its principal source of support might begin by examining the functions that this source previously sustained. Which of those functions remain indispensable? Where else are the necessary resources produced? What organizational changes would allow the project to incorporate them? Which new dependencies would emerge in the process?

The answers could lead to a considerably different system from the one originally envisioned. Some activities might be distributed among several organizations. Others might become embedded in educational, agricultural, cultural, or communicative practices. Professional expertise might be employed periodically to maintain interoperability, evaluate preservation conditions, or resolve technical problems.

Hydrothermal vent ecosystems provide no organizational blueprint for such arrangements. What they demonstrate is the possibility of biological productivity founded upon a metabolic pathway different from the one prevailing across most of the biosphere.

That possibility deserves attention in a professional field where the architecture of knowledge institutions is frequently determined by historically established mechanisms of support.

Two and a half kilometers beneath the Pacific, entire communities develop around chemical processes that would remain inaccessible to most familiar organisms. Their existence depends upon capacities that have evolved to make those processes biologically productive.

Perhaps some of the resources capable of sustaining fragile knowledge systems are already present in their surroundings. What remains to be developed is the infrastructure capable of making use of them.
